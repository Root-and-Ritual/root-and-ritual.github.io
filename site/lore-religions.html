World Religions: Origins & Summaries

A neutral, respectful overview of world religions: where each began and what it teaches, in no particular order. Dates are traditional or approximate.

← Back to the Lore Library · Updated Oct 20, 2026

Editorial context: these are unverified project notes, not scholarship. They have not been independently fact-checked, and recent claims follow a single research brief.

Entries

Christianity

Abrahamic faith centered on Jesus of Nazareth.

Origin / date: 1st century CE, Roman Judea.

Christians hold that Jesus is the Messiah and Son of God, and that his life, death and resurrection offer salvation. Most traditions affirm one God in three persons (the Trinity). Major branches include Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches, with scripture gathered in the Bible.

Islam

Abrahamic faith of submission to the one God (Allah).

Origin / date: 7th century CE, Mecca and Medina in Arabia.

Muslims hold that the Quran was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Gabriel. Practice rests on the Five Pillars: declaration of faith, prayer, almsgiving, fasting in Ramadan and pilgrimage to Mecca. The two largest branches are Sunni and Shia.

Judaism

Ancient monotheistic tradition and the people bound by its covenant.

Origin / date: Traditionally dated to the Bronze Age Middle East, with roots in the covenant with Abraham.

Judaism centers on one God, the Torah and a covenantal way of life. Later rabbinic tradition developed the Talmud and continuing legal and ethical interpretation. Observance ranges across Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and other communities.

Hinduism

Family of related traditions of the Indian subcontinent.

Origin / date: Developed from Vedic traditions, traditionally dated from roughly 1500 BCE onward.

Hinduism has no single founder or central authority. Common themes include dharma (duty), karma, samsara (rebirth) and moksha (liberation), approached through devotion, knowledge, action and meditation. Many deities, such as Vishnu, Shiva and Devi, are worshipped, often understood as aspects of one ultimate reality.

Buddhism

Path of awakening taught by the Buddha.

Origin / date: 6th to 5th century BCE, northeastern India (dates debated).

Buddhism teaches the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path as a way to end suffering. It does not rely on a creator deity. Main streams include Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana, spread across South, Southeast and East Asia.

Sikhism

Monotheistic tradition founded in Punjab.

Origin / date: Late 15th century CE, begun by Guru Nanak.

Sikhs follow ten human Gurus and the scripture Guru Granth Sahib. Core teachings stress devotion to one God, honest living, sharing with others and equality. The Khalsa order was established in 1699.

Jainism

Indian tradition centered on non-violence and spiritual discipline.

Origin / date: Ancient India; Mahavira is regarded as the 24th Tirthankara, traditionally dated to about the 6th century BCE.

Jains emphasize ahimsa (non-harming), truthfulness and non-attachment as the way to free the soul from karma. Teachers called Tirthankaras are honored as guides rather than creators. Monastic and lay communities both follow strict ethical vows.

Bahá'í Faith

Monotheistic faith emphasizing the unity of humanity.

Origin / date: 19th century Persia; Bahá'u'lláh declared his mission in 1863.

Bahá'ís believe in one God who guides humanity through a series of messengers, including Abraham, Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad and Bahá'u'lláh. Teachings stress the oneness of humanity, the harmony of science and religion, and the equality of women and men. The community has a worldwide administrative order.

Zoroastrianism

Ancient Iranian faith of Ahura Mazda.

Origin / date: Traditionally associated with the prophet Zoroaster; dating is debated, from about 1500 to 600 BCE.

Zoroastrianism teaches a world shaped by the struggle between truth (asha) and falsehood, with ethical choice at its center. Its sacred texts are the Avesta, and fire is a symbol of purity in worship. Communities today are small, notably the Parsis of India and groups in Iran.

Shinto

Indigenous religion of Japan centered on kami.

Origin / date: Prehistoric Japan; shrine traditions took shape over many centuries.

Shinto honors kami, sacred powers found in nature, ancestors and places. Practice focuses on shrines, purification, festivals and seasonal rites rather than doctrine. It has long coexisted with Buddhism in Japanese life.

Taoism

Chinese tradition of living in accord with the Tao (the Way).

Origin / date: Traditionally linked to Laozi and the Tao Te Ching, around the 4th to 6th century BCE (authorship debated).

Philosophical Taoism values simplicity, spontaneity and harmony with nature. A later religious Taoism developed temples, priesthoods, ritual and practices aimed at health and longevity. Both strands have shaped Chinese culture, medicine and art.

Confucianism

Ethical and social tradition from Confucius.

Origin / date: Around 551 to 479 BCE, China.

Confucian thought stresses virtue, family duty, education and good government. Key ideas include ren (humaneness) and li (proper conduct). Whether it is a religion, a philosophy or both is debated, but it has shaped East Asian societies for over two thousand years.

Chinese folk religion

Varied popular practice of ancestors, deities and local spirits.

Origin / date: Ancient China, developing over millennia.

Practice includes honoring ancestors, worshipping local and national deities, and seeking luck and protection through temples and festivals. It blends with Buddhist, Taoist and Confucian elements without a single organization. It remains widespread among Chinese communities worldwide.

Druze

Faith community of the Levant with a distinct, closed tradition.

Origin / date: 11th century CE, emerging in Fatimid Egypt from an Ismaili Islamic context.

The Druze hold monotheistic beliefs and the idea of reincarnation, and they value honesty and mutual support. Their teachings are kept within the community, and they do not accept converts. Most live in Lebanon, Syria, Israel and Jordan.

Yazidism

Kurdish religion with ancient roots.

Origin / date: Kurdistan region; the modern form is associated with Sheikh Adi in the 12th century CE.

Yazidis believe in one God who entrusted the world to seven angels, chief among them Melek Taus. Faith is passed on orally and through hymns, and the community has a strong tradition of endogamy. Yazidis have suffered serious persecution, including the 2014 attacks in Sinjar.

Mandaeism

Gnostic baptizing religion of Mesopotamia.

Origin / date: Early centuries CE, southern Mesopotamia (exact origin debated).

Mandaeans honor John the Baptist and revere Adam and other figures, with frequent ritual baptism in flowing water as a central practice. Their scriptures are in Mandaic, such as the Ginza Rabba. Communities in Iraq and Iran have shrunk, with a growing diaspora.

Samaritanism

Small Israelite tradition centered on Mount Gerizim.

Origin / date: Ancient Israel; the community traces itself to early Israelite tribes.

Samaritans follow their own version of the Torah, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and hold Mount Gerizim as sacred. They consider themselves guardians of the original Israelite religion. The community today numbers only a few hundred, living near Nablus and Holon.

Rastafari

Afro-Caribbean movement honoring Ethiopia and Haile Selassie I.

Origin / date: 1930s Jamaica.

Rastafari draws on the Bible, African pride and the memory of slavery and exile. Many adherents regard Haile Selassie I with reverence, though views of his role vary. Common themes include Zion, repatriation and living naturally, and the movement is closely tied to reggae music.

Cao Đài

Vietnamese religion blending several traditions.

Origin / date: Founded 1926 in southern Vietnam.

Cao Đài teaches that one supreme being is revealed through the world's religions, and honors figures such as Buddha, Confucius, Jesus and Laozi. Its Holy See is in Tây Ninh. Worship includes prayer, vegetarian days and communication practices in its early history.

Tenrikyo

Japanese new religion of the 19th century.

Origin / date: Founded 1838 by Nakayama Miki, Japan.

Followers believe that God the Parent created humanity to share a joyful life. Practice includes a ritual dance and service to others, and its center is Tenri City near Nara. It has branches in Japan and abroad.

Chondogyo

Korean religion with roots in the Donghak movement.

Origin / date: Founded 1860 by Choe Je-u, Korea.

Chondogyo teaches that the divine dwells within every person, summed up in the idea 'man is heaven.' It grew from the Donghak movement and took part in Korean reform and resistance history. It has followers in both Koreas.

Wicca and modern Paganism

Contemporary nature-centered religions.

Origin / date: 20th century Britain and beyond; Wicca was publicly introduced by Gerald Gardner in the 1950s.

Modern Pagan paths draw inspiration from pre-Christian beliefs, nature cycles and ritual. Wicca commonly honors a goddess and god and observes seasonal festivals. Claims of an unbroken ancient lineage are disputed by historians, and practice varies widely.

Heathenry (Asatru)

Modern revival of Germanic and Norse religion.

Origin / date: Organized from the 1970s onward, drawing on medieval Norse sources.

Heathens honor gods such as Odin, Thor and Freyja, along with ancestors and land spirits. Sources include the Eddas and sagas, interpreted today. Communities differ; many explicitly reject racist uses of the tradition.

Hellenic polytheism

Modern revival of ancient Greek religion.

Origin / date: Ancient Greece; the modern revival grew in the late 20th century.

Practitioners honor the Olympian gods and other Greek deities through offerings, festivals and household devotion, based on ancient texts and archaeology. The ancient religion itself declined under Roman Christianity. Modern groups include organizations in Greece and abroad.

Traditional African religions (Yoruba and Ifa)

Diverse indigenous belief systems of Africa.

Origin / date: Many regions and ancient origins; the Yoruba tradition is from West Africa.

In the Yoruba tradition, a supreme creator and many orishas guide life, and Ifa is a system of divination and wisdom verses. Africa has hundreds of distinct traditions, so no single description fits all. These beliefs also shaped religions in the Americas.

Vodou

Haitian religion with West African roots.

Origin / date: Developed in Haiti from West African, particularly Fon and Kongo, traditions mixed with Catholic elements.

Vodou honors a creator and spirits called lwa through music, dance and offerings. Community, healing and ancestors are central. Popular films have distorted it; it is a living religion practiced by many Haitians.

Candomble and Santeria

Afro-diasporic religions of Brazil and Cuba.

Origin / date: Developed from the Atlantic slave trade era, with Yoruba and other African roots.

Candomble in Brazil and Santeria (Regla de Ocha) in Cuba honor orishas, often paired with Catholic saints in the past as a form of cultural survival. Practice includes drumming, offerings and initiation. These are recognized religions with large communities.

Native American spiritual traditions

Many distinct Indigenous nations, each with its own beliefs.

Origin / date: Thousands of years in the Americas.

There is no single Native American religion; each nation has its own stories, ceremonies and relationship to land. Many emphasize kinship with nature and community responsibility. Some ceremonies are private, so this archive gives no ritual detail and recommends learning from tribal sources.

Australian Aboriginal Dreaming

Indigenous Australian understanding of creation and law.

Origin / date: Tens of thousands of years of continuous cultures in Australia.

The Dreaming, in various terms, refers to ancestral beings who shaped land and set law, with stories tied to specific places. Hundreds of language groups hold their own versions. Knowledge is often restricted by gender and initiation, so only general information is given here.

Unitarian Universalism

Liberal religious association with no required creed.

Origin / date: Formed in 1961 in the United States from Unitarian and Universalist roots.

Members draw on many sources, including world religions, reason and personal experience. Congregations affirm principles such as the worth of every person and the free search for truth. Beliefs among members vary widely.

Scientology

Movement addressing the spiritual nature and potential of the human being.

Origin / date: United States, 1950s.

Founded by science-fiction author L. Ron Hubbard; Dianetics published 1950; Church of Scientology founded 1953-1954 in the United States. It teaches that people are immortal spiritual beings (thetans) and uses 'auditing' toward a state called 'Clear'. It is recognized as a religion in some countries (e.g. US tax exemption in 1993) and disputed or restricted in others.

Spiritualism

Movement based on communication with the spirits of the dead.

Origin / date: Began in 1848 in New York with the Fox sisters.

Spiritualists hold that the dead can communicate with the living, often through mediums. It grew rapidly in the 19th century, and some mediums were exposed as frauds, including, by their own later admission, the Fox sisters. Spiritualist churches continue today.
